Runway is the number every founder should know without looking. It answers a simple question: if nothing changes, how many months until the money runs out? Here's how to calculate it properly and what to do with the answer.

Gross burn vs net burn

Gross burn is everything you spend in a month. Net burn is gross burn minus revenue. If you spend $10,000 and earn $4,000, your net burn is $6,000. Net burn is what drains the bank account, so it's the one that drives runway.

The simple formula — and why it's wrong

The textbook version is runway = cash ÷ net burn. With $60,000 in the bank and $6,000 net burn, that's ten months. The problem is that revenue and expenses rarely stay flat. If revenue grows 10% a month, burn shrinks and runway stretches; if you're hiring, expenses grow and runway shrinks. That's why our Runway Calculator projects month by month with separate growth rates for revenue and expenses.

Default alive or default dead?

The phrase, popularised in startup circles, asks: on your current trajectory — same growth, same spending, no new money — do you reach profitability before your cash runs out? If yes, you're default alive. If no, you're default dead, and you need to change something now rather than hoping a fundraise or a lucky month will save you.

Being default dead isn't a verdict; it's an early warning. The earlier you know, the more options you have.

How much runway is enough?

  • Fewer than 6 months: act immediately — cut costs, push revenue or raise.
  • 6–12 months: start fundraising or adjusting now; processes take longer than expected.
  • 12–18+ months: comfortable for most early-stage teams, allowing room to experiment.

Five levers that extend runway

  1. Raise prices. Often the fastest lever; most early products are under-priced. See how to price a SaaS product.
  2. Collect cash earlier. Annual plans, deposits and upfront payments improve cash flow without changing revenue.
  3. Cut non-core spend. Subscriptions, unused tools and experiments that haven't worked.
  4. Reduce churn. Keeping customers is cheaper than replacing them — check the impact in the MRR & LTV Projector.
  5. Add bridge revenue. Consulting, services or a paid pilot related to your product can fund the gap.

A worked example

A founder has $50,000, earns $2,000 a month growing 10% monthly and spends $7,000 a month growing 2% monthly. Net burn starts at $5,000 and shrinks as revenue grows, but projected month by month the cash runs out in about 11–12 months while revenue only overtakes expenses around month 18 — default dead. Push revenue growth to 15% a month and revenue catches expenses around month 12 before the money is gone — default alive. Drop growth to 5% and runway shrinks to under 10 months. Small changes in growth rate have outsized effects, which is why it pays to model several scenarios — these are the calculator's default inputs, so you can reproduce them yourself.
