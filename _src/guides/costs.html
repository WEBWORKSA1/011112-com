"How much do I need to start?" has no single answer — but it has a reliable structure. Every business has one-time costs to open the doors and monthly costs to keep them open. Get both right, add a buffer, and you know how much cash you need and how long it lasts.

The two buckets

One-time costs

  • Domain and branding: domain registration, logo, basic brand assets. Premium domains are a separate, optional investment.
  • Incorporation and legal: registering the business, basic contracts, terms and privacy policy. Costs vary a lot by country and structure.
  • Design and website: landing page, product UI, templates.
  • Product development: building the MVP — your time, contractors or tools.
  • Inventory and equipment: stock for e‑commerce, hardware, initial supplies.

Monthly costs

  • Software and AI tools: email, design, analytics, AI subscriptions.
  • Hosting and APIs: servers, databases, usage-based AI APIs — these scale with users.
  • Marketing: ads, content, sponsorships, events.
  • Insurance and accounting: bookkeeping, tax filing, liability cover where relevant.
  • People: contractors, part-time help or salaries — usually the largest line once you hire.

Add runway and a buffer

Total cash needed = (one-time costs + monthly costs × months of runway) × (1 + buffer). A 20% buffer is a sensible default for a first venture; complex products or hardware deserve more. Then check how long the cash actually lasts once revenue starts with the Runway Calculator.

Seven ways to spend less

  1. Validate before you build. A smoke-test page costs a fraction of an MVP — see our 7-day sprint.
  2. Use free tiers and pay-as-you-go pricing until usage forces an upgrade.
  3. Build with AI assistants and no-code for the first version; rewrite only what proves valuable.
  4. Pre-sell to fund development with customer money.
  5. Buy used equipment and order small inventory batches.
  6. Audit subscriptions every quarter — they creep.
  7. Pay for outcomes (fixed-scope contracts) rather than open-ended hours.

Where not to cut

Don't skimp on things that are expensive to fix later: proper contracts, clean bookkeeping from day one, security basics and a memorable, trustworthy domain name. A strong domain is one of the few early assets that can hold or grow its value.
