The 0‑1111‑2 Method

Six stages, each with a single job and a clear exit test. Don't move to the next digit until you've passed the one you're on.

By the 011112.com Editorial Team · Updated November 2026 · 9 min read

Ask a hundred founders why their first venture stalled and you'll hear the same story in different words: they built before anyone asked for it, launched to silence, or tried to scale something that wasn't yet earning. The problem is rarely effort. It's sequence.

The 0‑1111‑2 Method fixes the sequence. Read the number left to right: one 0 (the idea), four 1s (Validate, Build, Launch, Earn) and a final 2 (Scale). Each digit is a stage with a single job, a short list of activities, a metric that matters and an exit test — the evidence you need before moving on.

0 — Zero: find a problem worth solving

Every company starts at zero. The aim here is not a business plan; it's a sharp problem statement. Write one sentence in your customer's words: "I waste four hours every week reconciling invoices by hand." If you can't write that sentence, you have a solution looking for a problem.

  • Do: list ten problems you've experienced or observed at work; pick three you understand unusually well.
  • Ask: who has this problem, how often, and what do they do about it today?
  • Metric: number of distinct people who describe the same pain unprompted.
  • Exit test: a one-line problem statement and a named customer segment.

1 — Validate: replace opinions with evidence

Validation is cheap compared with building. Run 10–20 customer conversations focused on their past behaviour, not your idea. Then put up a one-page site that describes the outcome and asks for an email, a deposit or a pre-order. Our Idea Score tool helps you see which fundamentals are weakest before you spend money.

  • Do: interviews, a smoke-test landing page, a small paid or community traffic test.
  • Metric: conversion from visitor to sign-up or pre-sale.
  • Exit test: at least a handful of people who pay, pre-order or commit time — not just "that's cool".
Compliments are not commitments. A pre-order, a deposit or a signed letter of intent is.

1 — Build: the smallest thing that keeps the promise

Your MVP should deliver the single outcome your validation proved people want — nothing else. In 2026, AI coding assistants, no-code builders and API-first services let a solo founder ship in weeks what used to take a team months. Use the Startup Cost Calculator to set a budget ceiling before you start.

  • Do: define one core workflow, cut every "nice to have", ship to your validated users first.
  • Metric: time from first user action to first value ("time to wow").
  • Exit test: early users complete the core workflow without your help and come back.

1 — Launch: a sequence, not a day

Great launches are planned 30 days out. Warm your list, line up partners, prepare assets and pick a moment — a product directory, a community event or a commerce date such as 11.11. The Launch Checklist and Commerce Launch Calendar cover the details.

  • Do: pre-launch waitlist, launch-day posts across 3–5 channels, follow-up sequence for sign-ups.
  • Metric: activated users in the first 14 days.
  • Exit test: a repeatable way to get new users that doesn't depend on launch-day buzz.

1 — Earn: price, retain, stay alive

Revenue is the most honest feedback there is. Price on value rather than cost, measure churn every month and know your runway. Two numbers matter most: how long your cash lasts (Runway Calculator) and whether a customer is worth more than they cost to acquire (MRR & LTV Projector).

  • Do: test one price increase, add an annual plan, interview every churned customer.
  • Metric: net revenue retention and LTV:CAC (aim for 3:1 or better).
  • Exit test: you are "default alive" — on current trends, revenue covers costs before cash runs out.

2 — Scale: make it two

The final digit is a 2 because scaling is mostly about doubling what already works: a second acquisition channel, a second product for the same customer, a second geography, or a partner who brings distribution. Doubling something proven is far less risky than inventing something new.

  • Do: document your playbook, hire or contract for the bottleneck, add one channel at a time.
  • Metric: month-over-month growth with stable or improving unit economics.
  • Exit test: growth continues even when you step back for a week.

Why the number works as a map

The digits carry useful meanings beyond the stages. Zero and one are the alphabet of computing — every AI model and online business runs on them. Moving "from zero to one" is a widely used shorthand in startup culture for creating something new rather than copying what exists. And 11.11 — Singles' Day — has grown into the world's largest online shopping event, a reminder that well-timed launches matter. The 2 at the end is the payoff: partnership, duplication and growth.
