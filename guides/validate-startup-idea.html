How to Validate a Startup Idea in 7 Days

A day-by-day validation sprint: sharpen the problem, interview customers, launch a smoke-test page, drive targeted traffic and ask for a pre-sale — before you write code.

By the 011112.com Editorial Team · Updated November 2026 · 8 min read

Most ideas don't fail because they were built badly. They fail because nobody needed them badly enough. Validation is how you find out cheaply — in days, not months — whether a problem is real, painful and worth paying to solve. Here is a seven-day sprint you can run alongside a full-time job.

Day 1 — Write the problem, not the product

Describe the problem in one sentence, in the words a customer would use: "I lose two hours every Friday chasing unpaid invoices." Then name exactly who has it: "freelance designers billing 5–20 clients a month." Avoid describing your solution. If you can't write this sentence, go back to stage zero of the 0‑1111‑2 Method.

Finish the day by scoring the idea with the Idea Score and noting your three weakest factors. Those are the questions your interviews must answer.

Day 2 — Find 20 people to talk to

List places your customers already gather: niche communities, professional groups, forums, local meetups, your own network. Message people individually. Ask for 15 minutes to learn how they handle the problem today — not to pitch. A short, honest message gets far more replies than a polished one.

Day 3 — Run interviews about the past

People are poor predictors of their own future behaviour but reliable reporters of what they've already done. Ask:

  • "Tell me about the last time this happened."
  • "What did you do about it?"
  • "What have you tried? What did it cost?"
  • "What's the hardest part?"

Avoid "Would you use…?" and "Would you pay…?" — they invite polite yeses. Write down exact phrases; they become your landing-page copy.

Strong signal: they've already spent money or significant time on a workaround. Weak signal: "That sounds useful."

Day 4 — Build a one-page smoke test

Create a single page with a headline that states the outcome ("Get paid on time without chasing clients"), three benefits drawn from interview language, a simple explanation of how it works and one call to action. Make the action meaningful: join a waitlist, book a demo or — strongest of all — place a refundable pre-order. A simple static page is enough; it doesn't need a product behind it yet, as long as you're honest that it's coming soon.

Day 5 — Send targeted traffic

Aim for 200 or more relevant visitors. Share the page with the communities from Day 2 (following their rules), message interviewees, post a short build-in-public update and, if budget allows, run a small, tightly targeted ad test. Relevance matters more than volume: 200 of the right people beat 5,000 random clicks.

Day 6 — Ask for money

Email everyone who signed up and offer an early-adopter deal: a discounted first year, a founding-member price or a paid pilot. The goal is a commitment that costs them something. Even three pre-sales tell you more than three hundred likes.

Day 7 — Decide

SignalDecision
Clear pain, repeated phrases, 5–10%+ sign-up rate and at least a few pre-salesBuild the smallest version that keeps the promise
Pain is real but buyers won't pay or are hard to reachPivot the customer segment or pricing model
Little pain, low response, no commitmentsStop — keep the insight, change the idea

Benchmarks vary widely by market and traffic source, so treat these numbers as rough guides rather than rules. What matters is the direction of the evidence and whether people commit.

Common validation mistakes

  • Talking only to friends. They're kind, not representative.
  • Pitching instead of listening. If you talk more than 30% of the time, you're selling.
  • Counting compliments. Count commitments: time, money, reputation.
  • Validating a feature, not a problem. Confirm the pain first; the solution can change.
